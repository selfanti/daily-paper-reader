TaoLive Digital Avatar Agent Technical Report:
Training Agents to Evolve with Their Harness
TaoLive AIGC LLM Team
ABSTRACT
AI-powered digital avatar streamers in live e-commerce must answer product questions, interact with viewers,
and execute marketing strategies in real time, requiring low latency, frequent strategy updates, and accurate
yet effective replies. To enable frequent updates, we build an evolvable Harness whose Skills, Hooks, prompts,
and tools can be modified independently of model weights, allowing the system to iterate without retraining.
However, not all models can benefit from Harness Evolution. Models with weak generalization—such as those
trained via SFT on a single fixed Harness—overfit to the fixed configuration and cannot evolve with the Harness,
while large models with strong generalization have latencies too high for real-time interaction. We propose
Harness-Aware Training (HAT) to train a compact model that can evolve with the Harness. The core idea is to
expose the model to diverse Harness configurations during training so that it learns to understand the current
Harness rather than memorize a fixed version. HAT introduces Harness-State Augmentation (HSA), which
applies task-preserving transformations to Skill identifiers, Skill content, tool schemas, prompt structure, and
Hook functions, and organizes training into three stages: SFT with HSA (HSA-SFT), General Domain On-Policy
Distillation (General OPD), and Agentic RL with HSA (HSA-RL). Across four complementary evaluation sets,
the HAT-trained model reaches an average score of 94.8 on Live-Stream QA (base 80.3, strongest general LLM
93.0) and 94.6 on Harness-Variant QA (base 75.4). Crucially, it maintains 83.5 on IFEval, avoiding the significant
7.7-point drop typically observed after Fixed-Harness SFT. On a single NVIDIA H20 GPU (with optimization
enabled), it achieves a P50 latency of 3.407 s and P95 of 8.114 s. These results demonstrate that HAT produces a
compact agent that evolves with ongoing Harness changes under strict latency constraints.
1 Introduction
Digital avatars, also referred to as virtual humans are increasingly deployed across diverse interactive applications
including conversational assistants, customer service, education, and marketing [Cui and Liu, 2023, Allal-Chérif
et al., 2024, Yang et al., 2025]. Among these, live-streaming e-commerce represents a uniquely demanding scenario:
AI-powered digital streamers must interact with viewers in real-time, answer product questions, and execute
marketing strategies under strict latency constraints [Liu et al., 2025, Sun et al., 2025]. In our application, the digital
avatar’s replies are spoken publicly to the entire broadcast room, requiring the underlying agent to simultaneously
satisfy three conflicting requirements. First, ultra-low latency is essential, as long wait times severely degrade the
live viewing experience. Second, the agent demands high adaptability; campaign rules, compliance requirements,
and reply styles change frequently driven by the shifting preferences of operations teams and merchants. Third,
replies must be both accurate and effective—maintaining factual groundedness without making unsupported
claims (hallucinations), while successfully addressing viewer intent.
While a standard ReAct-style loop [Yao et al., 2023] enables reasoning and tool usage, it does not facilitate agile
strategy updates. In initial deployments, addressing new failure cases or modifying merchant-specific strategies
often required altering central prompts or control code, followed by a full service deployment or even model
retraining. This slow iteration cycle cannot keep pace with the high-frequency demands of live commerce. To

Skills (reply rules and strategies), Hooks (intermediate step validation and retry logic), a System Prompt Pipeline
(dynamic instruction assembly based on context), and a Tool Registry (commerce tool definitions). This architecture
allows the execution environment to evolve while policy model weights remain fixed—a practice we term Harness
Evolution, which is closely related to modular prompt and runtime optimization [Khattab et al., 2023, Hebbar
et al., 2026, Zhang et al., 2026, Lin et al., 2026, Qu et al., 2026, Ning et al., 2025]. Our system adopts this approach
through a developer-reviewed diagnose–edit–evaluate loop applied to these modules, enabling business behavior
to be adjusted within hours rather than requiring a lengthy retraining cycle. (Section 2.3).
Harness Evolution, however, changes the problem that the policy model must solve. Since every promoted
Harness version replaces the configuration the model was initially deployed against, the model no longer acts
inside a fixed execution environment but rather inside a continuous sequence of evolving environments: Skills are
rewritten, Hooks are added, prompts are reassembled, and tool schemas are renamed, all while the weights stay
unchanged. Robustness to Harness changes is therefore a property the policy model must possess rather than a
convenience we obtain for free; ironically, the more effective the evolution loop becomes, the more frequently the
model’s environment moves beneath it.
Which model can inhabit such a moving environment? Strong zero-shot models are the natural candidates,
as their general instruction-following lets them absorb a rewritten Skill or a renamed tool with no adaptation at
all—but their latency rules them out, with median end-to-end latencies exceeding 11 seconds for DeepSeek-V4
models in our tests, far beyond the real-time budget. The latency requirement thus forces a compact policy
model (e.g., Qwen3.6-35B-A3B), which in turn must be domain-trained, because its zero-shot accuracy does not
meet industrial standards. This is where the two mechanisms collide: Fixed-Harness SFT on the single Harness
configuration available at training time yields surface-form overfitting—the model memorizes specific Skill
names, tool names and prompt templates instead of reading the instructions actually placed in front of it—and
that configuration is precisely what Harness Evolution is designed to replace. A model trained this way becomes
stale exactly when the system evolves successfully. Our experiments make this cost concrete: Fixed-Harness SFT
improved domain-specific metrics but degraded general instruction-following ability (measured by IFEval [Zhou
et al., 2023]) by 7.7 points, the very capability needed to follow an unfamiliar Harness. Nor do existing alternatives
resolve the tension: test-time adaptation [Liang et al., 2024, Chen et al., 2026a] violates strict latency budgets,
while continual learning [Wang et al., 2024] re-pays a retraining cost for every Harness edit, which is unsustainable
at our update frequency.
We resolve this tension with Harness-Aware Training (HAT), which makes the Harness state an explicit part of
the training distribution rather than a fixed background assumption. Instead of fitting the policy to trajectories
collected under the single configuration ℎ𝑡that happens to be in production, we train over a distribution of Harness
states, so that the model learns to condition on whatever Harness it is currently given and can evolve together with
it. Concretely, we introduce Harness-State Augmentation (HSA) to apply task-preserving perturbations to Skill
content, tool schemas, prompt structure, and interaction constraints, and instantiate it in two training stages. The
complete pipeline comprises (1) SFT with HSA (HSA-SFT) using trajectories generated under varied Harness
configurations; (2) General On-Policy Distillation (General OPD) [Agarwal et al., 2024] to recover general
capabilities degraded during domain-specific SFT [Lu and Lab, 2025]; and (3) Agentic RL with HSA (HSA-RL)
within a production-informed live-room simulator, allowing the model to learn from the consequences of its
actions under shifting environmental conditions [Zhou et al., 2025, Jin et al., 2025].
Extensive evaluations demonstrate the effectiveness of our approach. On our real-world Live-Stream QA
benchmark, the selected 35B checkpoint achieves a 94.8 average score, surpassing both the untuned base model
(80.3) and strong zero-shot models. Crucially, it maintains robustness to evaluated Harness changes and avoids the
significant IFEval score loss observed after Fixed-Harness SFT.
Our Harness-based judge is calibrated against human annotations to ensure reliability. In controlled deployment
tests on a single NVIDIA-H20 GPU with MTP [DeepSeek-AI, 2024, Li et al., 2024, Chen et al., 2026b, Cheng
et al., 2026], the complete agent meets strict latency requirements, achieving a P50 wall-clock latency of 3.407s
and a P95 of 8.114s.

2 Digital-Avatar Harness Agent Architecture
The Harness Agent separates a slowly updated policy model from a rapidly evolving Harness state. This separation
lets operators change business behavior without retraining, but it also creates the central learning problem of this
paper: the policy must remain effective as the environment around it evolves. We first describe the application and
runtime, then formalize the non-training process as Harness Evolution.
2.1 Live-Streaming Digital-Avatar Scenario
We study a digital-avatar host that answers product questions, handles open-ended conversation, and invokes
external tools during live commerce. Public spoken responses are synthesized through TTS and avatar driving
for the whole room. Each request combines the viewer message with anchor speech, product data, link IDs, and
living-room status. The application demands low latency while business rules evolve weekly.
2.2 The Harness Agent Runtime
The Harness Agent runtime comprises four mechanisms. Skills are dynamically loaded behavioral modules for
Q&A strategy, tool choosing, and reply style. The System Prompt Pipeline assembles instructions from product
specifications, retrieved FAQ, living-room status, global boundary and active skill descriptions. Hooks validate
inputs, parameters, and output formats, triggering correction or retry when needed. Tool orchestration connects the
agent to external product, inventory, and commerce services. Because these modules are versioned independently
of model weights, a new skill or tool can be deployed as a harness change rather than a training cycle. And our
harness agent architecture is shown in Fig. 3.
2.3 Harness Evolution without Weight Updates
We use Harness Evolution to denote a controlled process that improves the agent by changing skills, prompts,
hooks, or tools while holding the model fixed. It is human-in-loop rather than fully autonomous: AI performs
failure clustering, diagnosis, and proposed edits; a developer confirms the plan, runs evaluation, and decides
whether to promote, revise, or stop. The complete operational protocol appears in Appendix B.

This section expands the task interface and runtime inventory summarized in Section 2. Evaluation roles and

Task Interface
The agent operates in live-streaming e-commerce, where a digital-avatar host responds to viewer comments
under the production latency bound. Each request contains the viewer’s comment, the anchor’s nearby utterances,
live-room and product context, applicable FAQ entries, and recent dialogue history. The output contains a
natural-language reply—either public speech sent to TTS and avatar driving or a private text response—plus the
structured tool-call trajectory used to produce it.
In single-comment mode, the runtime handles one viewer message independently; in multi-comment mode, it
aggregates related messages before responding. An available internal traffic summary groups the main viewer

3.2 Harness-State Augmentation (HSA)
Our working hypothesis is surface-form overfitting: when training data contains a single, fixed harness, the objective
permits shortcuts such as matching skill names by string similarity, selecting tools from memorized schema text,
or following instructions only in a familiar template. The core hypothesis is that changing the surface form of the
harness makes it harder for the model to rely on fixed names or templates as shortcuts. If a skill is renamed, its
description rewritten, and distractors injected, functional information about the skill becomes more useful for
routing than memorizing a single identifier.
We perturb the harness along five dimensions, each targeting a distinct aspect of surface-form dependence:
1. Skill identifier We expand the skill set with synthetic plausible skills and noise skills, randomly mask subsets
of existing skills, rename skills, and rewrite skill descriptions by a strong language model. These changes
reduce the usefulness of a fixed skill identifier.
2. Skill content. Within each skill, we paraphrase individual rules, randomly mask a subset, and reorder them.
This variation is intended to reduce reliance on rule position and exact wording.
3. Tool definitions. We rename tools, rewrite descriptions. This transformation makes name matching less reliable
while preserving the tool’s function.
4. System prompt. We reorder top-level instruction blocks and items within blocks, and perturb numeric
constraints (e.g., response length limits, maximum interaction rounds) within operationally valid ranges.
5. Hook. We design controlled variants of retry behavior, message structure, and hook-triggered modifications to
simulate the changes that the model may encounter after hooks are updated in production.

3.3 Simulated On-Policy Environment
HSA addresses environment coverage but operates on offline demonstration data: the model learns from teacher-
generated trajectories that always follow the optimal path. In real deployment, however, the agent must handle
situations that never appear in demonstrations—a tool call returns an unexpected error, a Hook intercepts and
forces a retry, or the agent selects a wrong Skill and must recover mid-conversation. Offline data provide no
learning signal from the model’s own failed trajectories and recovery attempts, because they contain only teacher
trajectories.
Moreover, the Harness Agent operates as a multi-step system: within a single user request, the model may
perform Skill selection, tool invocation, result interpretation, and response generation across multiple reasoning
rounds, with Hooks potentially triggering retries at each stage. Learning to navigate this dynamic interaction loop
requires the model to actually experience it during training—observing the consequences of its own actions rather
than imitating a teacher’s.
Environment Design. We construct a production-informed live-streaming simulator that implements the control-
flow elements needed for training: skills, tool executors, hooks, and bounded multi-round interaction. The simulator
consists of four components:
(1) Streaming input simulation. The environment generates realistic user inputs by sampling from a pool of
live-streaming scenarios: product inquiries, casual chat, purchasing intent, after-sales questions, and mixed-intent
bullet comments. Each input is contextualized with a simulated room state including current product listings,
historical conversation turns, and viewer action signals (e.g., adding to cart, joining the room).
(2) Harness Agent scheduling. The simulator implements the full Harness Agent control flow. Given the
model’s output at each reasoning step, the environment executes Skill routing logic (determining which Skills are
loaded based on the model’s selection), triggers Hooks at the appropriate checkpoints (input validation, parameter
checking, response format verification, Skill-loading enforcement), and manages the multi-round interaction loop
with a configurable maximum round limit.
(3) Tool execution simulation. When the model invokes a tool, the simulator executes the call against sandboxed
service replicas that return product details, pricing information, and inventory status from production catalog
snapshots. The simulator also injects controlled failures—timeout errors, malformed responses, and permission
denials—at calibrated rates to expose the model to error-recovery scenarios.
(4) Augmented Harness configuration. The simulated environment operates under augmented Harness states
produced by the method described in §3.2. This aligns the offline and on-policy stages to the same declared change
envelope.
Agentic RL Training. Within the simulated environment, we optimize the policy based on Group Relative Policy
Optimization (GRPO) [Shao et al., 2024]. For each prompt, 𝐺complete trajectory rollouts (group size) are sampled
and split into tool segments and reply segments. The reward signal is computed from four dimensions:
▶Accuracy: whether the final response contains factually correct information, judged during RL by a DeepSeek-
V4-flash Reward Judge;
▶Effectiveness: whetherthe response adequately addresses the user’s intentwithappropriate detailandactionability;
▶Tool Rationality: whether the tool invocation chain is logically sound and efficiently structured (no redundant
calls, correct parameter usage);
▶Skill Selection: whether the model correctly identifies the user’s intent and the current context to load the
appropriate Skills.

3.4 Training Pipeline
The complete training pipeline consists of three stages, each addressing a distinct capability gap:
Stage 1: SFT with Harness-State Augmentation (HSA-SFT). HSA-SFT constructs supervised fine-tuning
samples from real live-room interactions. Each original sample contains the viewer query, live-room context, and
product context, and is paired with a corresponding Harness which includes the available skills, tool definitions,
system prompt, and Hook configuration. To reduce the model’s dependence on the surface form of a single harness,
we first apply HSA to the Harness base state in Figure 2 before generating supervised trajectories. This produces
both the original Harness and multiple augmented Harness variants. HSA changes skill names and descriptions,
skill-rule order, tool descriptions, system-prompt structure, and the surface form of Hook messages and retry
behavior.
The teacher model then generates candidate trajectories under both the original harness and the augmented
harness variants, so the supervised signals cover multiple simulated environments. These candidate trajectories are
filtered according to Accuracy and Effectiveness. As a result, supervised learning exposes the student to the same
task semantics under different harness realizations, encouraging it to use the functional meaning of the current
Harness rather than fixed names or template matches.
Hooks in production may inform the model about formatting errors, parameter errors, or invalid outputs, and
ask it to revise. We want the model to correct itself when receiving a hook reminder, but we do not want it to learn

contain hook-triggered retries, we remove the intermediate failed outputs and hook interactions and keep only the

interpret hook reminders and recover after a failure.
Stage 2: General Domain On-Policy Distillation (General OPD). Motivated by prior practice showing that
On-Policy Distillation can improve general instruction-following ability [Lu and Lab, 2025], we add a General

4 Evaluation Design
This section describes the evaluation sources, the scoring metrics for each source, and the deployment replay
protocol used to measure actual latency and serving performance.
4.1 Evaluation Sets and Sources
We organize the evaluation around the requirements in the live-streaming application. For offline evaluation,
Live-Stream QA and Harness-Variant QA are both built from real live-room interactions and cover the main industry
scenarios in our application domain, with the available internal intent mix summarized in Appendix Table 1.
Live-Stream QA tests industrial reply quality, while Harness-Variant QA tests generalization and robustness to
changing Harness contexts. We also use a separate real live-room calibration cohort to align the Harness-based
Agent-as-a-Judge with human labels. To test broader tool and prompt robustness, we construct synthetic Tool
Robustness and Prompt Robustness subsets by expanding real-industrial scenario seeds with LLM generation and
consistency validation, as shown in Appendix E.3. We also evaluate general instruction-following ability on the
public IFEval benchmark [Zhou et al., 2023]. To test actual deployment latency and serving performance, we use a
fixed complete-Agent deployment replay. Table 2 summarizes the size, source, and role of each evaluation source.
4.2 Metrics and Scoring
For Live-Stream QA and Harness-Variant QA, we evaluate each response with Accuracy and Effectiveness using
the Harness-based Agent-as-a-Judge, following the LLM-as-a-Judge paradigm [Zheng et al., 2024]. Appendix E.1.
Accuracy is a binary metric that penalizes factual errors, unsupported product claims, false promises, and
capability overreach. Effectiveness measures whether the response addresses the viewer’s intent and provides
useful information, with scores of 0, 0.5, or 1. We report AVG as the per-sample arithmetic mean of Accuracy and
Effectiveness, computed before rounding.
For Tool Robustness and Prompt Robustness, we use task-specific rubric metrics. Tool Robustness uses ADR,
RC, and TC: ADR measures whether the response addresses the core viewer query, RC measures whether the
response uses the required information returned by tools, and TC measures whether the expected tool is called.

5.6 Human Blind-Test Protocol and Results
We conduct a human blind test to compare the modular Harness runtime with a ReAct-style baseline on real
live-streaming requests. We draw a stratified 100-request blind-test set with a fixed random seed so that its scenario
composition follows the full pool.
For each request, both systems run independently on the same input. The record preserves the user input,
dialogue history, complete reasoning and tool-use trace, and final response from both systems. During annotation,
the two responses are randomly assigned to Model A and Model B, and the annotator is not shown the system
identity. The annotator chooses one of three labels: Harness better, ReAct better, or tie. Non-tie decisions require a
short reason. After annotation, all 100 examples are reviewed for data consistency and labeling consistency.
Table 9 reports the preference distribution. Among the 36 examples with a clear preference, Harness wins 35,
corresponding to a 97.2% non-tie win rate. A two-sided binomial test against equal preference gives 𝑝< 0.001. The
only ReAct-preferred example is an ambiguous short comment, where Harness matches an irrelevant FAQ-style
response while ReAct gives a safer transitional reply. We therefore treat this case as an isolated fallback-policy
failure rather than a recurring disadvantage.
Table 10 summarizes the attribution ofHarness wins. The largestsources ofimprovementare inputunderstanding,
output reliability, and scenario-appropriate behavior, which together account for 80.0% of the Harness-preferred
cases. These categories include correctly separating live-room events from viewer questions, avoiding unsupported
claims or false promises, handling low-information comments without forced product promotion, and using
available tool evidence more appropriately. The blind-test result is consistent with the automatic Judge evaluation
and provides an additional human preference check on real live-streaming requests.

6 Deployment and Production Lessons
A production live-streaming policy must simultaneously keep end-to-end P95 below 15 seconds, retain open-ended
instruction following after domain training, and accommodate weekly changes to Skills, tools, and prompts.
The selected HAT checkpoint meets the latency requirement in the controlled low-concurrency replay, while
the reported Fixed-Harness SFT checkpoint loses general-set and prompt-robustness points. HAT and Harness
Evolution are therefore operated as two independently versioned paths.
6.1 Evolution-Aware Release Process
Policy checkpoints change through HAT, whereas Skills, prompts, Hooks, and tools change through Harness
Evolution (Section 2.3). A release manifest binds the policy checkpoint, Harness commit, tool schemas, fallback
thresholds, and evaluation-set versions. Every proposed change runs the business regression suite and the general-
capability gate before traffic promotion. Public-channel changes receive stricter review because one spoken
response reaches the whole room. The non-monotonic trajectory in Figure 2 motivates an explicit early-stop rule:
when long-tail fixes begin creating cross-category regressions, operators retain the last passing version rather than
accumulating more rules.
Three production gates
Promote a version only when it passes the 15-second latency bound, the business regression suite, and the
general-capability gate. Policy and Harness versions have independent switches, allowing a regression to be

6.2 Failure Containment and Recovery
The runtime uses defense in depth rather than relying on the policy alone. Parameter Hooks reject malformed tool
calls before execution; tool-result Hooks convert service errors into typed observations; the Skill-loading and
output-format Hooks force a bounded retry; and the loop terminates after four model rounds. A first-call timeout
of 3 s, later-call timeout of 2 s, and 10 s trajectory budget trigger the compact fallback route. If recovery still fails,
the system returns a conservative response or suppresses the public reply instead of inventing product facts.
Three recurrent high-severity errors shaped release checks: unsupported store or product claims, false claims
that a human is driving the avatar, and tool calls that repeatedly fail or omit required parameters. Product, price,
inventory, and promotion statements must be grounded in the corresponding tool result. An unsupported-claim
Hook blocks unverifiable attributes, guarantees, discounts, and delivery promises; identity rules require disclosure
that the host is an AI avatar. Guardrail hits, retries, fallbacks, and suppressed replies are logged with the model and
Harness versions for incident review.
6.3 Low-Concurrency Serving Policy
The serving configuration uses the selected task-trained policy on one H20 with MTP enabled. The application
is provisioned for one or two simultaneous requests per replica rather than high-throughput batch inference. A
separate SGLang operational trace is consistent with this workload shape: sampled decode log intervals have mean
2.03 and median 1 running request, with a P95 of 7 and no queued requests in the captured window. These are
log-sample-weighted scheduler observations, not a request-weighted production concurrency distribution.
The controlled replay therefore treats concurrency 1–2 as the decision-relevant range. In that range, MTP gives
the selected checkpoint 1.69×/1.52× higher client-observed decoding throughput while maintaining 8.114/9.047 s
P95 and 100% 15-second attainment. The concurrency 4–8 runs are stress tests: the selected checkpoint’s decoding
benefit falls to 1.27×/1.19×, and MTP no longer improves P95. This boundary motivates provisioning additional
replicas rather than treating MTP as a universal high-concurrency throughput optimization.

7 Related Work
Agent architectures and Harness Evolution. ReAct [Yao et al., 2023] interleaves reasoning and action in a
single prompt; Toolformer [Schick et al., 2024] teaches models to invoke external tools; Reflexion [Shinn et al.,
2023] adds within-episode self-correction. TPTU-v2 [Kong et al., 2024] improves task planning and tool use in an
industrial agent system, while DSPy [Khattab et al., 2023] compiles declarative modules into optimized prompts.
RIVA [Abuzakuk et al., 2026] addresses configuration-drift detection in infrastructure through cross-validating
agents; our problem is complementary because the configuration being changed is the agent’s own execution
environment. Harness Evolution makes that editable runtime—Skills, prompt assembly, Hooks, and tools—an
explicit release process and a training-distribution object.
Prompt, schema, and agent-evaluation robustness. LLM behavior can vary under semantically equivalent
prompt templates [Zhuo et al., 2024], and tool selection is sensitive to edits in natural-language tool descrip-
tions [Faghih et al., 2025]. SMART [Qian et al., 2025b] further shows that agents must learn when not to invoke a
tool. These findings motivate varying prompt and tool interfaces rather than evaluating a single frozen surface form.
Stateful benchmarks such as 𝜏-bench [Yao et al., 2024] and ToolSandbox [Lu et al., 2025] evaluate multi-turn
interaction and intermediate state; ECom-Bench [Wang et al., 2025] adds dynamic user simulation for e-commerce
customer support. Our T3 instead targets broad live-stream avatar interactions and complements the controlled
Harness variants in T2.
Agent post-training. SFT-based agent training constructs trajectory data for instruction following and tool
use [Zeng et al., 2024, Chen et al., 2023, 2024]; rejection-sampling and self-taught variants filter successful roll-
outs [Zelikman et al., 2022, Yuan et al., 2023]. RL methods optimize policies through environment interaction [Putta
et al., 2024, Xi et al., 2024]; ToolRL [Qian et al., 2025a] studies structured reward design for tool selection and
application, while GRPO [Shao et al., 2024] provides the group-relative objective used here. These agent RL
approaches build on the reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) paradigm [Ouyang et al., 2022] that
first demonstrated effective policy optimization for instruction-following language models. RandomWorld [Sullivan
et al., 2025] procedurally generates interactive tools and compositional tasks. Our environment keeps the real
business ontology and randomizes the editable Harness around it. On-policy distillation [Agarwal et al., 2024]
anchors general behavior after domain SFT. Unlike work whose primary novelty is a reward, credit-assignment rule,
or optimizer, our contribution lies in formulating the execution environment as a changing training-distribution
object and evaluating a controlled combination of established mechanisms in production.
Training under distribution shift. Domain adaptation aligns source and target distributions [Wang et al., 2022],
continual learning handles sequential tasks without forgetting [Wang et al., 2024], and test-time adaptation adjusts
models at inference [Liang et al., 2024]. Recent agent-specific test-time adaptation learns environment format
and dynamics through deployment-time interaction [Chen et al., 2026a]; it is a complementary option when
changing weights or probing the environment online is acceptable. Harness-State Augmentation (HSA) is a
domain-randomization instantiation [Tobin et al., 2017]: it trains across controlled variation so later conditions
within the declared change envelope are more likely to lie within learned support. The adaptation here is to
symbolic, executable, and coupled agent configuration: a valid change may require consistent edits across Skills,
prompts, tools, Hooks, and message logic. T2 evaluates in-family changes, while T3 evaluates broader in-domain
scenario transfer; neither constitutes unseen-family validation.

8 Conclusion
We presented Harness-Aware Training (HAT), a systems-and-training methodology for the tension between
versioned Harness change and policy stability in live-streaming digital-avatar agents. It treats Harness-state
variation as a training-distribution design problem within an explicitly defined change envelope.
Our work makes four contributions. First, Harness Evolution versions runtime changes separately from policy
updates through modular Skills, Hooks, prompts, and tools, and exposes the training problem created by a moving
execution environment. Second, HAT treats Harness-state variation as part of the training distribution through HSA
and a three-stage pipeline with HSA-SFT, General OPD, and HSA-RL. Third, scoped offline sets, a human-aligned
evaluation panel, and a controlled complete-Agent deployment replay separate checkpoint quality evidence from
serving-route behavior. Fourth, the ablations provide practical training insights: General OPD mainly recovers
instruction-following ability after Fixed-Harness SFT, while HSA is most effective when introduced during SFT.
In our study, the HAT-trained compact model reaches 94.8 Live-Stream QA AVG and does not exhibit the
general-set drop observed after Fixed-Harness SFT. On one H20 with MTP enabled, the controlled replay reaches
8.114 s P95 at concurrency 1 and 9.047 s at concurrency 2, with 100% 15-second attainment; MTP increases
client-observed decoding throughput by 1.69×/1.52× over the same checkpoint without MTP. Overall, these results
show that HAT can produce a latency-feasible compact agent while preserving the adaptability needed for evolving
production Harnesses.

B Harness Evolution Protocol and Diagnostics
B.1 Evolution Loop
Each Harness Evolution stage holds model fixed and changes only editable runtime modules. The evolution loop
contains five steps:
1. AI diagnosis. Analyze the latest evaluation result, cluster the top failure causes, attach representative bad cases,
and propose root causes.
2. Human confirmation. A developer reviews the proposed skill, prompt, hook, or tool changes, then accepts,
modifies, or rejects the plan.
3. AI-assisted editing. Apply the confirmed changes to skill definitions, prompt assembly, Hooks, and tool logic
while preserving a versioned configuration snapshot.
4. Human-triggered evaluation. Run inference and scoring on the dev-set.
5. Regression check and planning. Compare paired category changes, inspect new regressions, and let a human
choose promotion, rollback, another evolution stage, or stopping.
The stopping rule is operational: stop when the remaining errors are sparse long-tail cases and local rule additions
are more likely to cause cross-category regression than systematic improvement.
B.2 What Changed across Evolution Stages
The process report attributes the largest Accuracy gains to negative-feedback chat (+60.7 points), after-sales and
returns (+53.3), and purchase/payment cases (+33.3). The largest Effectiveness gains occur for emotion expression
(+27.3), membership or gift benefits (+18.8), and positive-feedback or already-purchased cases (+14.3). These
category values are engineering diagnostics rather than pre-specified inferential analyses.
After Evolution 2, the report records 36 remaining Accuracy failures across 16 categories and 35 Effectiveness
failures without a dominant systematic cluster. Evolutions 3–4 show why “more rules” is not equivalent to better
evolution: targeted long-tail fixes create interactions across Skills, Hooks, and global instructions. This observation
motivates configuration diversity during training and regression-aware early stopping during production evolution.

E.2 Offline Scoring and Implementation
Accuracy is binary; Effectiveness uses 0, 0.5, and 1; AVG is the per-sample mean of the two computed before
rounding. T3 reports Tool and Prompt robustness separately, and IFEval uses its official prompt- and instruction-level
evaluators. The policy is Qwen3.6-35B-A3B. HSA-RL uses GRPO with Accuracy, Effectiveness, Tool Rationality,

model comparisons use identical set versions and the same Final Evaluation Judge.
E.3 T3 Robustness Subset Construction
T3 contains two synthetic robustness subsets generated from a real-business scenario seed library: Tool Robustness
(𝑛= 1,532) and Prompt Robustness (𝑛= 491). The seed library is built from live-streaming e-commerce scenarios
and records the product context, viewer intent, possible tool needs, and reply constraints for each scenario family.
Construction pipeline. We use a shared seed-driven generation pipeline and instantiate it into two orthogonal
branches. The seed pools are extracted from real live-streaming business data, including product context, FAQ-style
knowledge, dialogue history, and historical tool-use traces. For each item, an LLM generator expands a sampled
business seed into a natural viewer comment, live-room or product context, and the expected behaviors to be
evaluated. The generated item is then converted into the Harness input format with the corresponding tool
configuration, instruction configuration, simulated tool-return map, and evaluation rubrics. We apply rule-based
and LLM-based consistency checks to ensure that the query, context, tool returns, instructions, and rubrics are
mutually consistent. Items that fail validation are discarded and regenerated.
The Tool Robustness subset focuses on tool selection under distractor tools, while the Prompt Robustness

subset additionally constructs instruction-following prompts to test whether the model can satisfy valid prompt
constraints. The corresponding metrics are Tool Called (TC), Response Contains (RC), and Addresses Core Query

Prompt Robustness. Each final item may contain multiple rubrics. AVG is the fraction of passed rubrics computed

F.1 Deployment Inference Benchmark
Protocol and Workload Shape
The deployment set contains 110 cases, with 10 warm-up cases excluded and 100 requests retained. The client runs
on an office-network host Harness, real RAG and MCP services, a four-round Agent limit, and a 5-second tool
timeout. Generation uses temperature 1.0, top-𝑝0.95, and a 16,384-token initial generation limit with configured
step-up to 65,536. The reported local configurations use one H20, BF16, TP=PP=1, and no quantization. A
separate FP8 trial did not yield a material additional serving gain under this workload, so the reported runs retain
BF16. The model’s NextN MTP module is the draft head; the serving engine consumes its predictions through an
EAGLE-style speculative generation and verification path. Thus, NextN identifies the model-side draft module and
its weights, whereas EAGLE describes how those draft predictions are used during inference.
Wall latency starts at client dispatch and ends when the Agent produces its final response. TTFT is measured to
the first streamed chunk of the first model call; it includes client networking, request queueing, and server work, so
it is not pure kernel prefill time. Per-call Decode TPS is completion tokens divided by call latency minus TTFT,
and the table reports the arithmetic mean of these per-call ratios. This estimator is sensitive to completion length
and timer granularity for very short calls; it is not an intrinsic cross-checkpoint kernel-speed measurement. We do

Technical Report · August 2026
not report client-derived Prefill TPS, TPOT, or inter-token latency, and we do not equate this per-call metric with
SGLang’s interval-level aggregate generation throughput. Execution success means the Agent returned normally;
it does not score response correctness. Table 18 records the workload presented to each C=1 route. Calls and
tokens vary because each model chooses its own Agent trajectory. API usage supplies token counts for the remote
routes; the self-hosted routes use the serving tokenizer. Token counts from different tokenizers are useful workload
descriptors but are not identical measures of Chinese text length.
The output distributions explain why cross-checkpoint mean Decode TPS should be read as workload-shaped.
At C=1 with MTP disabled, the Base route generates 115 tokens per call on average (median 78; P95 327.8),

interval is 1 ms, producing a 9,000-token/s ratio; consequently its mean Decode TPS is 195.42 while its median is
162.63. HAT has mean/median values of 160.30/159.71. We therefore attribute the raw Base–HAT mean gap
chiefly to generated-length and trajectory distributions together with short-call timing granularity, not to intrinsic
decoding speed. The within-checkpoint MTP Off–On comparisons remain the primary acceleration evidence.
Under MTP On, the residual cross-checkpoint difference also coincides with the higher acceptance statistics
reported in Table 20, so it is not attributed to length alone.

record GPU count, TP/PP, dtype, MTP parameters, and known scheduler settings. Request-level records retain wall

request-weighted concurrency and queue distributions, exclusion rules, and raw telemetry.
